CRISPR–Cas systems are RNA-guided immune systems found in bacteria and archaea that have been repurposed for programmable nucleic acid targeting and editing. Despite their broad utility, widely used CRISPR effectors remain constrained by size, targeting requirements, variable activity and specificity, and potential immunogenicity and cellular toxicity limiting their full application in diagnostics and therapeutics.

Our laboratory explores the natural diversity of CRISPR–Cas systems to identify new effectors with distinct and advantageous properties. Using computational mining, we discover previously uncharacterized systems and investigate their molecular properties and mechanisms of action through biochemical, structural, and functional studies. We then apply AI-assisted engineering, including protein and genomic language models, to optimize promising candidates and develop them into diagnostic and therapeutic tools. Our current research focuses on sensitive and multiplexed RNA detection, sequential RNA recording in single cells, efficient large-fragment DNA knock-in and deletion, and precise RNA editing and knockdown. Ultimately, we aim to expand the CRISPR toolbox and enable more precise and tailored approaches to diagnostics and therapeutics.

Current Projects

RNA Detection

Sensitive and multiplexed in vitro RNA detection via reprogrammed tracrRNAs.

Knock-In

Precise and highly efficient large insertion in eukaryotic cells.

RNA Targeting

Precise and non-cytotoxic RNA knock-down.

Knock-Out

Large genomic deletion in bacterial and eukaryotic cells.

Molecular Recording

Sequential RNA recording to trace molecular histories inside cells.

Novel Systems Mining

Discovering and engineering novel CRISPR and bacterial defense systems.
